A dash cam is cheap insurance against a he-said, she-said crash. A front-only camera covers what happens ahead of you,
but many of the incidents drivers actually deal with happen behind or beside the car: rear-end collisions, hit-and-runs
in parking lots, and someone backing into you and driving off. A front-and-rear (two-channel) system covers both, and a
three-channel system adds a camera facing the cabin.

This guide ranks five front-and-rear dash cams for different drivers and budgets, scores each one out of 10, and
explains which specs matter. If you want the full explanation of resolution, sensors, parking modes and memory cards,
read our [dash cam buying guide](/dash-cams/buying-guide/).

The parking features are strong for the price: three modes, including buffered event recording that keeps 15 seconds
before and 30 seconds after something bumps or approaches the car. You'll need VIOFO's HK4 hardwire kit (sold separately)

## How to choose a front and rear dash cam

### Rear resolution matters more than front "4K"

Almost every two-channel camera now claims 4K at the front. The bigger difference is often the rear camera. A 2K
(1440p) rear camera, like the VIOFO's or Thinkware's, captures noticeably more detail than a 1080p one, which helps
read the plate of a car that's tailgating or hits you from behind. Also check the sensor: a true 4K sensor beats an
upscaled one.

### Night performance comes from the sensor, not the resolution

Most crashes and break-ins that people need footage of happen in poor light. Look for a modern low-light sensor (Sony
STARVIS 2 is the current benchmark) and HDR or WDR. A sharp 4K daytime image doesn't mean you'll read a plate at night.

### Parking mode: decide before you buy

If you want the camera to protect a parked car, budget for constant power: a hardwire kit to the fuse box, an OBD-II
cable, or a dedicated dash cam battery pack. Then compare how each camera detects incidents:

- **Buffered recording** keeps a few seconds from before the event, so you see the car approaching, not just the
  aftermath.
- **Radar** (Thinkware U3000) watches for movement with less power draw than continuous motion detection.
- **Time-lapse and low-bitrate modes** record continuously while using less storage.

### Two channels or three?

Choose three channels if you carry passengers for work, want a record of what happens inside the car, or need
interior evidence after a break-in. Otherwise, two channels give you most of the protection with less heat, simpler
wiring and lower storage needs.

### Screen or app?

Screenless cameras (BlackVue, Thinkware) hide behind the mirror and are less tempting to thieves, but every setting
change and playback happens on your phone. A screen (REDTIGER, Vantrue) makes quick checks easier.

### Storage and heat

Use a high-endurance microSD card rated for continuous recording, and check the maximum size each camera supports.
Cameras with supercapacitors, rather than lithium batteries, generally cope better with hot cars.

## Installation tips

- **Mount the front camera behind the rear-view mirror**, inside the windshield wiper sweep, where it won't block your
  view.
- **Route cables along the headliner and down the A-pillar trim.** Be careful near side-curtain airbags; tuck the cable
  behind the airbag, not in front of it.
- **Measure the rear cable run before you buy.** Long SUVs, vans and pickups may need a longer rear cable than the one
  in the box.
- **Set the date, time and GPS overlay**, then check the first recordings to be sure the angle captures the road, not
  your hood.
