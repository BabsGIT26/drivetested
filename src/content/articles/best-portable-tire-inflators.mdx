A portable tire inflator is one of the most useful things you can keep in a car. Tires lose air slowly over time and
faster in cold weather, and underinflated tires wear out sooner, hurt fuel economy and handle worse. With an inflator in
the trunk you can top up at home in a couple of minutes instead of hunting for a gas-station air machine that works.

This guide ranks six inflators for different drivers, from glovebox-sized cordless units to a battery-clamp compressor
for big truck tires. Each one gets a score out of 10, and where independent inflation times exist we show them, because
claimed speeds on the box vary a lot.

  independent test results. Inflation times in the table come from Car and Driver's test, which timed each unit from

## How to choose a portable tire inflator

### Speed: look for independent times, not claims

Manufacturers quote inflation times on different tire sizes and pressure ranges, so claims aren't comparable. Independent
tests that use the same tire for every unit are much more useful, which is why we show Car and Driver's times above.
Bigger tires take longer, so if you drive a truck or large SUV, favor the faster units.

### Power source

- **Cordless (built-in battery):** the most convenient. Check how many top-ups it gets per charge and how long it holds a
  charge in storage.
- **Removable tool battery:** great if you already own that brand's batteries (Milwaukee, DEWALT, Ryobi and others).
- **12V plug or battery clamps:** never runs out while the engine runs. Clamp-on compressors can draw more current and
  usually inflate faster.
- **Both:** a cordless unit with a 12V backup cord gives you the best of both.

### Auto shut-off and presets

Set the target pressure, start it, and walk away. Auto shut-off prevents over-inflation and is the single most useful
convenience feature. The VIAIR 88P is the only pick here without it.

### Hose length and connector

A longer hose makes it easier to reach the valve, especially on trucks. Screw-on brass chucks seal well but take longer to
attach; clamp-on chucks are faster. The Slime's 15-foot hose is unusually generous.

### Max pressure and duty cycle

Most car tires need 30 to 40 PSI, so a 100+ PSI rating is plenty. Duty cycle tells you how long the pump can run before it
needs to cool down, which matters when you're filling four large tires in a row.

## How to top up a tire

1. **Check the door-jamb placard** for the right pressure. Don't use the "max pressure" on the sidewall.
   [NHTSA's tire guidance](https://www.nhtsa.gov/equipment/tires) explains why.
2. **Measure cold** when you can, before driving.
3. **Set the target**, attach the chuck firmly, and start the pump.
4. **Let it rest** if it gets hot, and respect the duty cycle.
5. **Double-check with a separate gauge**, then refit the valve cap.

An inflator won't fix a leak. If a tire keeps losing air, have it inspected.
