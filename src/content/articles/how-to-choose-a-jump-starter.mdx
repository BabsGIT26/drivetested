A portable jump starter is a battery pack with clamps that gives your car's battery enough power to crank the engine.
It's the fastest way to fix the most common reason a car won't start, and you don't need a second vehicle. But the specs
on the box can be confusing, and some of the biggest numbers matter the least.

This guide explains each spec in plain English, in the order you should think about them. If you just want our
recommendations, jump to the [best portable jump starters](/jump-starters/best-jump-starters/) or the
[best jump starters with air compressors](/jump-starters/best-with-air-compressor/).

<Callout title="The short version">
  1. Choose a model whose **engine rating** is above your engine size, with extra headroom for diesel or cold weather.
  2. Compare **battery capacity in watt-hours (Wh)**, not peak amps.
  3. Check **clamps and cable length**, then **safety features**, **charging** and **extras**.
</Callout>

## Step 1: Match the engine rating to your vehicle

Every reputable jump starter lists the largest gas and diesel engines it's designed for, such as "8.0L gas / 6.0L diesel."
This is the most useful number on the box, because it's the manufacturer's own statement of what the unit can handle.

Find your engine size in the owner's manual, on the engine cover, or from your vehicle's specs online. Then choose a model
rated comfortably above it. Give yourself more headroom if any of these apply:

- **You drive a diesel.** Diesels have high compression and need more cranking power, which is why ratings list diesel
  separately and lower.
- **You live somewhere cold.** Cold reduces battery output and thickens engine oil, so starting takes more energy.
- **Your vehicle is older or cranks slowly.** Worn starters and long crank times need sustained current, not just a burst.
- **You might help other drivers.** A bigger unit can rescue a friend's truck, not just your hatchback.

As a rough guide, a compact car with a 2.0-liter four-cylinder can be served by a small unit like the
[NOCO GB40](/jump-starters/best-jump-starters/#noco-gb40), while a V8 pickup or a diesel calls for something like the
[NOCO GBX155](/jump-starters/best-jump-starters/#noco-gbx155).

## Step 2: Ignore peak amps (mostly)

Peak amps is the maximum current a unit can deliver for an instant. There's no industry standard for how it's measured, so
a pocket-sized pack can claim 3,000 or 4,000 amps. Real starting current is much lower. In MotorTrend's testing, a combo
unit rated at 2,000 peak amps measured about 371 amps of actual output on a load tester, and that was still enough to
start modern vehicles.

So treat peak amps as marketing. Two units with the same engine rating but different peak-amp claims are usually much more
alike than the numbers suggest.

## Step 3: Compare battery capacity in watt-hours

Capacity decides how many jumps you get per charge and how useful the unit is as a power bank. Watt-hours (Wh) are the
honest comparison. Many brands list milliamp-hours (mAh) instead, measured at the lithium cell's internal voltage of about
3.7 volts, which makes the number look big.

To convert: **Wh = mAh × 3.7 ÷ 1,000**. A 20,000mAh pack is about 74Wh; a 10,000mAh pack is about 37Wh.

Independent tests often find usable capacity below the claim. TechGearLab measured the NOCO GB40 at 15.57Wh usable from 24Wh
claimed. A rough way to choose:

| Capacity | Good for |
| --- | --- |
| Under 30Wh | One or two jumps on a small engine, then recharge |
| 30–60Wh | Several jumps on most cars and SUVs, plus phone charging |
| 60Wh and up | Big engines, repeat jumps, real power-bank use |

## Step 4: Check clamps and cable length

In independent tests, clamp and cable complaints come up more than anything else. Short leads and stubby clamps make it hard
to reach batteries tucked under covers, against the firewall, or in the trunk. Car and Driver measured lead length in its
test and singled out the Clore JNC770R's long cables and curved clamps for buried batteries, while TechGearLab praised the
NOCO GBX155's long cables and versatile clamps.

Look for strong springs, copper teeth that bite on terminals and jump posts, and enough cable to set the unit down securely
instead of holding it.

## Step 5: Understand the safety features

Modern lithium jump starters include protections that old jumper cables don't:

- **Reverse-polarity protection** stops power if you connect red and black the wrong way round.
- **Spark-proof design** keeps the clamps dead until they're connected correctly.
- **Short-circuit, over-current and temperature protection** protect the unit and your car.

Most units also have a **boost or manual override** mode for batteries so flat the unit can't detect them. Override
bypasses some protections, so use it only as the manual describes and double-check polarity first.

## Step 6: Lithium or lead-acid?

| | Lithium | Lead-acid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size and weight | Small and light (1–7 lb) | Heavy (often 15–30 lb) |
| Holds charge | Months | Needs regular top-ups |
| Cold weather | Output drops; many won't charge below freezing | Tolerates cold better |
| Extras | USB-C, power bank, light | Sometimes air compressor or AC charger |
| Best for | Keeping in the car | Shops, farms and home garages |

For most drivers, lithium is the right choice. Lead-acid boxes like the
[Clore JNC770R](/jump-starters/best-jump-starters/#clore-jnc770r) make sense if you jump a lot of vehicles.

## Step 7: Charging, ports and extras

- **USB-C Power Delivery** recharges faster from a common laptop-style charger. Check whether a wall charger is included.
- **Power-bank output** keeps phones and tablets running on trips and in outages.
- **A 12V output** can run accessories such as tire pumps.
- **A built-in air compressor** saves space; see our
  [combo guide](/jump-starters/best-with-air-compressor/) for the trade-offs.
- **Displays** that show battery level and errors are easier to trust than a few LEDs.
- **Pre-heat for cold weather** (like the HULKMAN Alpha85S) helps the battery deliver power in deep winter.

## Step 8: Warranty and storage

Lithium batteries wear out over time, so warranty and support matter. Warranties among our picks range from one to two
years. Check the storage temperature range, because a car's interior can get very hot in summer and very cold in winter.
Recharge after every use and check the charge every few months.

## How to use a jump starter safely

1. Read your owner's manual for jump points and any special steps (some cars specify remote terminals).
2. Turn the ignition, lights and accessories off.
3. Connect the **red clamp to positive (+)**, then the **black clamp to negative (–)** or an unpainted metal ground point.
4. Turn the jump starter on and wait for the ready indicator.
5. Start the car. Don't crank longer than the manual allows, and rest between attempts.
6. Once it starts, disconnect **black first, then red**, and recharge the jump starter.
7. Drive for a while, or use a battery charger, to recharge the car's battery. If it keeps going flat, have the battery
   and charging system tested.

Never jump a battery that's cracked, leaking, swollen or frozen. On hybrids and EVs, use only the 12-volt jump points the
manual describes.
