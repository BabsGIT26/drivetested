A jump starter with a built-in air compressor covers the two most common reasons a car leaves you stuck: a dead
battery and a low tire. Combining them saves trunk space, and modern lithium versions are small enough for a
glovebox or door pocket. The catch is that combo units vary a lot. Some are a strong jump starter with a weak pump
attached; others inflate quickly but drain their battery doing it.

This guide explains what the numbers on the box actually mean, picks five combos worth buying for different needs,
and walks through how to use one safely.

## How to choose: what the specs actually mean

### Peak amps: the least useful number on the box

Peak amps is the maximum current a unit can deliver for a split second, and there's no single industry standard for
how it's measured. That's why a small pack can claim 3,000A or more. Real starting current is far lower. As noted
above, MotorTrend measured about 371 amps from a unit rated at 2,000A peak. That was enough to start modern vehicles,
but it shows how far apart the marketing number and real output can be.

**What to use instead:** the manufacturer's **engine-size rating** (for example "8.0L gas / 6.0L diesel"). Choose a
model rated comfortably above your engine. Diesels, large V8s, old batteries and cold weather all demand more, so
give yourself headroom if any of those apply.

### Battery capacity: Wh beats mAh

Capacity is often listed in milliamp-hours (mAh), usually measured at the internal cell voltage of about 3.7 volts,
not at 12 volts. **Watt-hours (Wh)** is the more honest comparison: Wh = mAh × 3.7 ÷ 1,000. A 16,000mAh pack works
out to about 59Wh, and a 12,000mAh pack to about 44Wh.

Capacity matters more on a combo unit than on a plain jump starter, because **the compressor uses far more energy than
a jump start does**. If you expect to fill tires regularly, especially large ones, favor more Wh.

### Max PSI vs. airflow

**Max PSI** (120, 150, 160) only tells you the highest pressure the pump can reach. Most car and SUV tires run
around 30–40 PSI, so any unit here has plenty of pressure for them. **Airflow** (liters per minute, L/min) is what
decides how fast a tire actually fills. Higher airflow matters most for big truck and off-road tires.

### Auto shut-off, presets and gauge accuracy

Look for a digital display where you set a target pressure and the pump stops automatically. It prevents
over-inflation and lets you step away. Built-in gauges can drift, so it's worth checking against a separate
quality tire gauge occasionally.

### Lithium vs. lead-acid

- **Lithium** (most models here): small, light and able to hold a charge for months. Sensitive to extreme heat and
  cold, and many won't charge below freezing.
- **Sealed lead-acid** (DEWALT DXAEJ14): heavy and bulky, but tough and often paired with garage-friendly features.
  It needs regular top-up charging to stay healthy.

### Charging and power-bank features

USB-C Power Delivery (PD) input means faster recharging and one less proprietary charger to lose. Higher wattage (60W
or 65W vs. 30W) recharges faster. Most units also work as power banks for phones, which is handy on a road trip.

### Clamps, cables and build

Short or bulky clamps are the most common complaint in independent tests. If your battery sits under a cover, is
tucked against the firewall or uses remote jump posts, clamp shape and cable length matter more than an extra
1,000 peak amps.

### Warranty and support

Warranties here range from one to two years. A lithium battery is a wear item, so a longer warranty from a brand
with reachable support is worth something.

## How to jump-start a car safely with a portable jump starter

Always follow your vehicle's owner's manual and the jump starter's manual first. Some vehicles specify remote jump
points or a particular ground location. The general process looks like this:

1. **Check the battery first.** Don't jump a battery that's cracked, leaking, swollen or frozen. Make sure the area is
   well ventilated and keep sparks and flames away.
2. **Turn everything off.** Ignition off, lights and accessories off, and the jump starter off or with its clamps
   unplugged.
3. **Connect red to positive (+).** Attach the red clamp to the battery's positive terminal or the vehicle's positive
   jump post.
4. **Connect black to ground.** Attach the black clamp to the negative terminal or to an unpainted metal ground point
   your owner's manual specifies, away from the battery.
5. **Power on and wait for the "ready" indicator.** Most units won't send power if the clamps are reversed. Fix any
   warning before continuing.
6. **Start the engine.** Crank for no more than the time the manual allows, and rest between attempts as instructed.
   If it doesn't start after a few tries, stop. You may have a different problem.
7. **Disconnect in reverse order.** Black clamp first, then red, then switch the unit off and recharge it.

<Callout title="Safety notes" tone="safety">
  - **Boost/override mode** bypasses some safety checks so it can start a fully dead battery. Confirm polarity
    before you press it.
  - These units are for **12-volt systems only**. On hybrids and EVs, use only the 12-volt jump points described in
    the owner's manual.
  - **Store within the temperature range** on the label, and recharge after every use and every few months in storage.
  - If a lithium pack ever **swells, gets unusually hot or smells**, stop using it and recycle it properly.
</Callout>

## How to inflate a tire with a combo unit

1. **Find the right pressure** on the placard inside the driver's door jamb (or in the owner's manual). Don't use the
   "max pressure" printed on the tire sidewall. [NHTSA's tire guidance](https://www.nhtsa.gov/equipment/tires)
   explains why.
2. **Check tires cold** when you can, before driving or after the car has sat for a few hours. That's when readings
   are most accurate.
3. **Set the target pressure**, attach the hose firmly to the valve, and start the pump. Let auto-stop do its job.
4. **Let it rest.** Compressors get hot. Respect the unit's run-time limit and let it cool before the next tire.
5. **Recheck with a separate gauge** if you have one, and refit the valve cap.

A compressor won't fix a puncture. If a tire keeps losing air, have it inspected and repaired or replaced.

## Who shouldn't buy a combo

- **You only need one function.** A dedicated jump starter or a dedicated inflator can do its single job better for
  the same space. See our guides to the [best portable jump starters](/jump-starters/best-jump-starters/) and the
  [best portable tire inflators](/tire-inflators/best-tire-inflators/).
- **You regularly air up big off-road tires.** A dedicated high-airflow 12V compressor powered from the vehicle will
  be faster and won't drain a small battery.
- **You have a 24V or commercial vehicle.** These picks are 12V only.
