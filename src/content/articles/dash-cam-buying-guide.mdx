A dash cam has one job: capture clear, usable footage of what happened. Clear means you can read a license plate, see the
color of a traffic light, and show the time and speed. Usable means the file was saved, wasn't overwritten, and survived
summer heat. Spec sheets focus on resolution, but several less flashy features decide whether a camera delivers when it
counts.

This guide walks through each spec in order of importance. If you'd rather skip to recommendations, see our ranked list
of the [best front and rear dash cams](/dash-cams/best-front-and-rear/).

<Callout title="The short version">
  Prioritize a **modern low-light sensor with HDR**, **front and rear coverage**, **reliable storage** and a
  **parking mode that suits where you park**. Treat "4K" and huge field-of-view numbers with some skepticism.
</Callout>

## 1. Channels: how many directions do you need?

- **Front only (1 channel):** the cheapest and simplest, but blind to rear-end crashes and parking-lot hits.
- **Front and rear (2 channels):** the sweet spot for most drivers. Covers the two most common crash directions.
- **Front, cabin and rear (3 channels):** for rideshare and delivery drivers, or anyone who wants a record of what happens
  inside the car. The cabin camera usually uses infrared so it can see passengers at night.

More channels mean more heat, more wiring and faster-filling memory cards, so don't buy a third camera you won't use.

## 2. The image sensor matters more than resolution

The sensor is what turns light into video, and it determines how well the camera sees at night, which is when many
incidents happen. Sony's STARVIS sensors are the industry benchmark, and **STARVIS 2** is the current generation, with
better low-light sensitivity and built-in HDR support. All of our top three picks use STARVIS 2 on both or all cameras.

Two cameras with the same "4K" label can produce very different night footage depending on the sensor and lens. If you can,
watch unedited night samples from owners before you buy.

## 3. Resolution: true 4K vs. upscaled

Higher resolution helps you read details further away, like the plate of a car two lanes over. But the resolution claim
has to match the sensor. An independent analysis of the budget REDTIGER F7N found its front sensor is a 2.5K (1440p) Sony
IMX335, with the advertised 4K produced by upscaling, which adds file size without real detail. The current Amazon listing
now describes the front camera as "4K/2.5K."

Practical guidance:

- **Front:** true 4K or 2K on a good sensor.
- **Rear:** 2K (1440p) captures noticeably more than 1080p, which matters for reading the plate of a car behind you.
- **Cabin:** 1080p with infrared is enough.

## 4. HDR and WDR: for headlights and shadows

Night scenes have extreme contrast: bright headlights and dark surroundings. Without high dynamic range (HDR) or wide
dynamic range (WDR), plates wash out in the glare or disappear into shadow. HDR combines exposures to keep both readable.
Look for HDR on the rear camera too, not just the front.

## 5. Field of view: wider isn't always better

A wide lens (140° to 170°) captures more lanes and the sides of intersections, but it also makes distant objects smaller and
can distort the edges. Around 140° to 160° is a common balance. Don't choose a camera on field of view alone.

## 6. Parking mode: match it to where you park

Parking mode records incidents while the car is off. It needs constant power (a hardwire kit, OBD-II cable or dash cam
battery pack). The main types:

| Mode | How it works | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impact (G-sensor) | Wakes and records after a bump | Long parking with minimal battery use |
| Motion detection | Records when something moves in view | Driveways, quieter streets |
| Buffered recording | Keeps a few seconds before the trigger | Seeing who approached, not just the aftermath |
| Time-lapse / low bitrate | Records continuously at reduced quality | Continuous coverage with less storage |
| Radar | Built-in radar detects approach with low power draw | Busy lots and street parking |

The [VIOFO A229 Pro](/dash-cams/best-front-and-rear/#viofo-a229-pro-2ch) offers buffered event recording that saves 15
seconds before and 30 seconds after an event. The [Thinkware U3000](/dash-cams/best-front-and-rear/#thinkware-u3000) adds
radar for longer low-drain coverage.

## 7. GPS, Wi-Fi and the app

- **GPS** stamps location and speed on your footage, which helps with insurance claims and disputes.
- **Wi-Fi (preferably 5GHz)** lets you download clips to your phone quickly instead of pulling the memory card.
- **Cloud features** (like BlackVue Cloud or Thinkware Connected) can send alerts and live view when the car is parked,
  but they need an internet connection, often through an LTE module and a data plan.
- **Voice control** lets you save a clip or take a photo without touching the camera.

A good app matters because many modern cameras have small screens or none at all.

## 8. Storage and memory cards

Dash cams overwrite old footage in a loop and lock clips when the G-sensor detects a crash. To keep that working:

- Use a **high-endurance microSD card** rated for continuous recording.
- Check the **maximum card size** each camera supports (512GB on the VIOFO A229 Pro, 1TB on the Vantrue N4 Pro S).
- **Format the card in the camera** every month or two.
- Size up for more channels and higher resolution. Three 4K-class cameras fill a card much faster than one.

## 9. Heat: capacitor vs. battery

A car parked in the sun can get extremely hot inside. Many dash cams use a **supercapacitor** instead of a lithium battery
to keep the clock and save the last file, because capacitors tolerate heat better. Mounting the camera behind the mirror,
out of direct sun on the body, also helps.

## 10. Screen or screenless

Cameras with screens are easier to set up and check on the spot. Screenless cameras are smaller and less noticeable, which
can deter theft, but every setting and playback happens in the app.

## Installation basics

1. Mount the front camera **behind the rear-view mirror**, inside the wiper sweep and out of your line of sight.
2. Tuck the power cable along the **headliner and down the A-pillar**, routing it behind (not in front of) side-curtain
   airbags.
3. Measure the **rear cable run** before you buy, especially on large SUVs, vans and trucks.
4. For parking mode, use a **hardwire kit with low-voltage cutoff** or an **OBD-II cable** that's powered with the ignition
   off.
5. Set the **date, time and GPS**, then review the first recordings to check the angle.

## Legal and privacy notes

Recording video from your car is generally legal in the U.S., but states regulate windshield mounting and recording
audio without consent. Keep the camera out of your line of sight, and consider turning off cabin audio, or tell passengers
it's on, especially if you drive for a rideshare service.
